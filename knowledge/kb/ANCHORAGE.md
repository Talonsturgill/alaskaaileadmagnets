# ANCHORAGE — the operating context the translation lands in

Read `README.md` in this directory first. `knowledge/ALASKA_TRANSLATION.md`
owns the translation RULES and the twin map. This file owns the operating
CONTEXT: why a build that works in Ohio behaves differently here, so the
translation says something a local operator recognises instead of something
generic with the word Alaska in it.

Everything here is a PRIOR. Any figure that ships is fetched this run.

---

## THE ONE THING THAT MAKES THIS MARKET DIFFERENT

Almost every operating disadvantage here compounds into the same shape:
**mistakes are more expensive and slower to fix.**

A part that is wrong ships by barge or by air. A hire that does not work out
comes from a thinner labour pool. A season that goes badly does not get made
up next quarter, because there is no next quarter. A vendor whose support is
Pacific time answers at 6am.

**Why this is the core of the pitch, honestly framed:** the value of getting a
decision right the first time is structurally higher here than in the lower
48. That is a real argument for tools that reduce error, shorten cycles, and
let a small team cover more ground. It is not fear and it should never be sold
as falling behind.

## THE SECTOR STRUCTURE, in operator terms

The mid-market employer base clusters into a small number of recognisable
shapes. Dana is one of these.

- **Trades and mechanical contractors.** Field techs, a dispatch board, an
  estimating desk, and a bidding calendar. Recognises P5 (dispatch), P10
  (quoting), P6 (field reports) instantly.
- **Freight, logistics and fuel distribution.** Trucks, yards, barge and air
  schedules, and lead times that punish being wrong about demand.
- **Seafood processing.** Extreme seasonality, a workforce that multiplies for
  a season, quality control at speed, and compliance reporting.
- **Healthcare groups and clinics.** Documentation burden, front-desk load,
  and recruiting into a market that is hard to recruit into.
- **Engineering, architecture and permitting-heavy firms.** Document review,
  submittals, and proposal work as the actual bottleneck.
- **ANCSA corporations and their subsidiaries.** Often the largest and most
  sophisticated operators in the state, frequently running federal contracting
  practices with heavy proposal and compliance load. Distinctive governance
  and a shareholder obligation that shapes decisions.
- **Tourism and lodging.** A whole year of revenue between May and September,
  and a call and booking load that spikes accordingly.
- **Utilities and co-ops.** Inspection, maintenance, and long asset lives.
- **Mining and resource support.** Remote sites, connectivity constraints,
  heavy safety reporting.

**Use segments, never named targets.** Named local companies appear only as
public-record sector anchors or when their story is already public. This is a
hard privacy rule, not a style preference.

## THE STRUCTURAL FACTS THAT CHANGE A BUILD

**Seasonality is not a modifier here, it is the shape of the year.** A
business that triples in July should model usage-priced tools on July. A
ninety day benefits ramp that starts in April lands after the season it was
bought for. This single fact should appear in most translations, because it
changes both the cost and the timing of the decision.

**The labour market is the why-now.** Sustained net out-migration, a large
nonresident share of the seasonal workforce, and genuine difficulty filling
skilled roles. The honest frame is not "replace people." It is: this is how an
operation does more with the workers it can actually get, and how it stops
losing the institutional knowledge that walks out with a retirement.

**Distance and logistics.** Lead times are long and freight is expensive.
Anything that reduces rework, wrong orders, or a second trip is worth more
here than the vendor's ROI model assumes.

**Connectivity varies enormously.** Anchorage is well connected. Remote sites,
vessels and camps are not. Any build that assumes constant bandwidth needs an
offline story, and "does it work at the site" is a real qualifying question.

**Small IT teams.** Most mid-market operations here run lean on internal
technical staff. This is precisely why the qualifying filter demands bought
and configured rather than built. A story requiring a data team is not
copyable here, and saying so is useful information.

**Regulatory texture.** Federal contracting rules for ANCSA subsidiaries.
Fisheries management and observer requirements. Healthcare regulation. Safety
regimes in marine, aviation, and resource work. Alaska bans finfish farming,
so aquaculture computer-vision cases are analogies here and must be labelled
as analogies.

**Time zone.** One hour behind Pacific, four behind Eastern. Vendor support
windows and implementation scheduling are a real friction and worth naming
when a case's timeline depended on vendor responsiveness.

## WHAT MAKES A TRANSLATION LAND

The YOUR VERSION slide fails when it is generic. It works when a local
operator reads it and thinks the writer has been in a building like theirs.

**Do:**
- Name the specific segments with this exact bottleneck
- Describe the local version of the build in local nouns: the dispatch board,
  the barge schedule, the season, the yard, the permit file
- Give a cost range appropriate to mid-market scale here, as a range
- Name where it does NOT apply, in plain words, as scoping rather than
  disclaimer
- Say what would have to be true first, locally, before this could start

**Do not:**
- Say "Alaska businesses" as though that were a segment
- Import a lower 48 cost figure without saying it is a lower 48 figure
- Assume a national vendor supports operations here without checking
- Use the labour situation as a threat

## THE LIMIT, every time

Every translation names at least one honest limit. The usual candidates:
scale (the case's volume is far above local volume), data (the local version
of this data does not exist yet), regulation, season (the ramp lands wrong),
connectivity, or the plainest one of all, that the local answer is ordinary
software and not AI at all.

Naming the limit is what makes the rest believable. It is the most persuasive
paragraph on the slide, and it is not a disclaimer.
