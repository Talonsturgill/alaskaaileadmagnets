## 0. THE PORTABLE THING

Strip the county government off this case and what is left is one sentence an
Anchorage operator says out loud every week.

Per Wisconsin Public Radio, the operations supervisor put it this way. By
virtue of being a 24/7 department, they had just become a default for anything
related to municipalities or police departments (c31).

That is the whole translation. **A line that is staffed around the clock
becomes the default number for everything, because it is the only one that
picks up.** The people trained for the expensive call end up answering the
cheap question. And per WEAU, the shape of the cheap question is exactly what
you think it is. How do I pay a parking ticket. What time is court. Can I talk
to an inmate (c33).

The desk being described is at least four dispatchers per shift, per Milwaukee
Magazine (c37), open around the clock, taking roughly 105,000 non-emergency
calls a year, per the county (c08). **Dana does not recognise a county. Dana
recognises that desk instantly.**

## 1. SEGMENTS

Ranked by strength of the rhyme. Segments only. No named local businesses
appear as targets anywhere in this dossier.

**1. Mechanical, plumbing, electrical and heating contractors with a 24/7
service line.** The closest rhyme in the market, close on all three axes.

*Same bottleneck physics.* One after-hours number exists. It is staffed for the
no-heat call at 40 below. It receives "what do I owe you," "when is my tech
coming," "do you guys do boilers," "can you resend the invoice," and the person
holding the phone is the one you need free when the real call lands.

*Same call stream.* Every one of those routine calls resolves to either a
different desk or a link. That is precisely the two-outcome shape the county's
supervisor described to WEAU, forward the caller directly to the agency they
need, or text them how to request the information (c24).

*Same seasonality, and it is starting right now.* Freeze-up and boiler-season
service calls ramp in September. The winter friction is on the record in the
operator's own words. Per WEAU, Lynch describes taking a non-police call,
having to put that caller on hold to answer a 911 call that could be for an
accident, then picking the caller back up and having them start their story
over again if a different dispatcher answers (c34). Read that as a Tuesday in
January at a mechanical contractor with two dispatchers and a storm. It is the
same paragraph.

**2. Utility, co-op and pipeline member service and outage lines.** The outage
number is the number the public knows, so it is the number the public uses for
billing, meter reads, new service, tree trimming and locate requests. During an
event the same line carries both the outage and the "why is my bill high." High
destination count, which is what earns the build.

**3. Property management and commercial building operators.** The 24/7
maintenance line becomes the default for lease questions, rent payment,
parking, lockouts and noise. Then a pipe freezes at 2am. Highest ratio of
routine to real in the segment list, which is the ratio that makes the
economics work.

**4. Freight, air cargo, fuel distribution and marine dispatch desks.** The
dispatch phone is on around the clock because weather does not keep hours. It
absorbs "where is my shipment," "what is the barge doing," "can I move the
delivery," landing on people whose actual job is diverting freight.

**5. Clinic groups, behavioral health and veterinary with an after-hours triage
or answering service.** Genuine rhyme, hardest scoping. Fifth not because the
bottleneck is weaker but because the cost of a wrong route is clinical, which
pushes the human gate earlier and makes the hot-word list serious safety work.

**6. Tour operators, lodges, charter fleets and guide services.** Not a 24/7
year-round line, but between May and September the reservations number is the
default for everything. The seasonal-swell rhyme is on the record, and it is a
vendor page so it carries its label. Per Aurelian's own case study, La Crosse
serves over 110,000 residents in a population that swells during the school
year thanks to two universities and a technical college (c52). A county whose
call load has a season, buying a tool priced on call volume. That is the Alaska
tourism operator's exact problem.

**7. Borough, municipal and tribal-organisation service desks.** The literal
twin, ranked last deliberately, because it also carries the hardest version of
the failure mode in section 5.

## 2. THE HONEST RUNG

**Rung 4. A workflow.** Labeled analysis, our judgement applied to verified
mechanism facts, not something any source states.

The vendor calls it an agent. The press calls it an agent. It is not rung 5,
and saying so is the useful part rather than a dunk, because **rung 4 is the
correct answer for this job.**

Every step was drawn by a person before the phone ever rang.

- It answers the non-emergency line (c22).
- It works out what the caller needs and collects the one fact routing depends
  on. On a parking citation question it asks where the ticket was issued and
  forwards the call to that specific municipality (c23). That single sentence
  is the whole build in plain English.
- It finishes one of two ways: forwards the caller directly to the agency they
  need, or sends a text on how to request the information (c24).
- It escalates on a keyword (c25). **A person wrote that list.** The machine is
  not judging urgency, it is matching words somebody typed in.
- Anyone can get a human at any time (c26).

Nothing on any page fetched this run shows the system choosing its own path or
its own tools. That is a workflow, and it is the right product.

**The demo question this hands Dana.** Ask a voice vendor who decides the order
of the steps. If the answer is "we did, at build time," you are looking at a
rung 4 workflow. That is usually what you want. Do not pay agent prices for it.

### The local version of the build, in local nouns

A heating and mechanical contractor, this September, before boiler season.

- The 24/7 emergency service number does not change. Nothing touches it. Per
  Ozaukee County's sheriff describing the same product, 911 calls go straight
  to a dispatcher (c27). Your equivalent is the gas smell and the no-heat call
  at 40 below, and it stays on a human.
- The routine line, or an option on the main line, is where this points.
- It asks the one question routing depends on. Which building. Which unit.
  Which invoice. Then it warm-transfers, or texts the link.
- **Your service manager writes the hot-word list.** Gas. Smoke. No heat. Water
  everywhere. Carbon monoxide. Anything on the list goes to the on-call tech
  immediately, no interpretation.
- Anybody who asks for a person gets a person.
- Your service manager, not your IT contractor, meets the vendor every other
  week and reads transcripts (c28).

### When the honest rung is lower, and it often is

If your after-hours line takes twenty calls a night and fifteen are the same
three questions, **the honest answer is rung 1 and it is not AI at all.** A
better phone tree. Published hours. An online payment page. A text auto-reply.

La Crosse earned rung 4 on two facts: roughly 105,000 non-emergency calls a
year (c08), and many destinations, because the correct answer genuinely varies
by which municipality issued the ticket (c23). **Volume plus a branching
destination map is what pays for a model. One line and three answers is a
phone tree.**

## 3. COST CLASS

### Lead with this, because no vendor will say it

**The price is usage-based, so the bill goes up as the thing works harder.**

Per Milwaukee Magazine, Ava costs La Crosse, based on call volume, around
$67,000 its first year and $81,000 its second (c10, c11, c14). That is not a
price increase. **That is the product succeeding, invoiced.**

For an operation whose call volume triples in July and idles in February, this
is the single most transferable warning in the entire case. **Model your July,
not your February.** A quote built on a shoulder-season month is a quote for a
business you do not run.

Ask for the price at three volumes: quietest month annualised, average, and
peak month annualised. Ask whether there is a floor you pay in February and a
cap you hit in July.

## 4. WHAT IT TAKES

### Data readiness, in local terms

This build needs almost nothing Dana does not already have. The bad news is
that the one thing it needs is usually in somebody's head.

**A written destination map.** The routing decision in La Crosse is "which
municipality issued the ticket, forward there" (c23). Your version is which
yard, which branch, which PM, which crew lead, which billing clerk. If that map
exists only in the memory of whoever normally answers the phone, **that gap is
the project** and the AI part comes after it.

**Somewhere to send people.** The text-message path (c24) only works if there
is a destination. If the answer to "how do I pay this" is "call us," a voice
product cannot fix it and you have found a rung 1 job you should do first.

**A hot-word list**, written by a person, not learned (c25). This list is the
safety design of the whole build and it takes an afternoon and the right
afternoon person.

**Your callers' actual vocabulary.** See section 5. Most likely to be
underestimated here.

### People, and who owns it

The named human on the record is Cory Lynch, operations supervisor, title
confirmed identically across three independent outlets (c30). **Not the IT
director. The person who runs the desk.** And he is the one meeting the vendor
every other week and working the dispatcher feedback (c28). That is the
ownership evidence most case studies leave out, and it is the difference
between a deployment and a pilot that dies in ninety days.

Local read: the owner is your service manager, your dispatch supervisor, your
front-office lead. Somebody whose own week gets better. **If it is assigned to
whoever handles the network, it will be configured correctly and abandoned.**

### Ramp, scoped exactly

The only La Crosse duration that survived verification: the county says the
platform underwent several months of intensive training before implementation
(c18), and it took its first call on 5 May 2025 (c01). **That is preparation
time. It is not decision-to-working, and no downstream artifact may present it
as one.**

The one glimpse of method comes from a different agency and is named as such.
Waukesha County ran the system for about 15 hours in a live environment during
training, then turned it off to review results and make adjustments before
going further (c50). **That is the shape of a sane local pilot.** Run it live
for a shift, turn it off, read every transcript, fix, repeat.

**The Alaska timing note, and it is late August, which makes it urgent.**
Calendar-year budget season starts now. If the line you want covered is a
May-to-September reservations line, several months of preparation in front of
go-live means a decision made in April lands after the season it was bought
for. If the line is a freeze-up service line, the ramp is already tight.

## 5. WHERE IT DOES NOT TRANSLATE

Four limits. All scoping. The first two are the most useful things here.

### Limit 1. Containment is not resolution, and this deployment cannot tell you the difference.

Per the county's own count, roughly 65 percent of the 41,000 calls the system
took were handled without a dispatcher having to answer the phone (c03, c04).

**Read the base carefully.** That is 65 percent of the 41,000 calls the system
took, not of the roughly 105,000 routine calls the county gets in a year. Those
two numbers do not share a base and may never be multiplied together.

**Read the verb carefully.** It counts calls that **ended**, not calls that got
**answered**. A caller who got exactly what they needed and a caller who gave
up confused are the same row in that report.

Here is the finding. **No resolution, satisfaction, callback or complaint
figure for this deployment exists on any of the eleven pages fetched this run,
including the vendor's own case study** (c53). The only caller feedback
anywhere is two unattributed anecdotes on a sales page.

That is not a reason to dismiss the case. It is the question Dana should carry
into every voice AI meeting from now on. Ask for the containment rate, the
resolution rate, and what happens on transfer, and insist on all three. **This
is a well-documented, genuinely working, three-outlet-verified deployment and
it cannot answer question two. If this one cannot, assume the demo in front of
you cannot either.**

### Limit 2. Deployment quality is the variable, not model quality, and Alaska's version is harder.

The same product, same vendor, went sideways at a neighbouring Wisconsin county
in week one. The sheriff said they went live 24/7, saw they were having some
issues, and decided that until they got that figured out they needed to just
back off (c44). They cut it to two hours a day, with human dispatchers handling
anything after (c45).

The failure modes are the interesting part. People are not specific enough,
they often ask for a sheriff when they mean a deputy, and sometimes they are
not calling the right county (c46). And the system can be confused by
background noise such as shouting, music and other conversations (c47).

**Both of those are context problems, not intelligence problems. The vocabulary
of your callers is part of the build.**

This is a pullback and not an abandonment, and saying otherwise would be
dishonest. The article's own headline is that after a few hiccups Ava is
settling in, and the sheriff says the vendor has been great to work with and
very receptive when issues surface (c48).

**Now translate the failure.** A caller in Wisconsin says sheriff when they
mean deputy. A caller here says a village name, a borough name, a road-system
place name, a mile marker, a subdivision, or a fish camp, with two spellings
and three pronunciations, on a line where the caller may not use the name your
dispatch system uses. **That is a genuinely harder version of the exact thing
that broke Ozaukee, and it is the item to test before signing, not after.**

Note the pairing across the two agencies. Lynch names background noise as the
thing a human catches (c43). Ozaukee found background noise is exactly what the
machine trips on (c47). **Same sentence, two agencies. That is the honest
boundary of the entire category.** Locally, the noisy caller is on a plant
floor, a boat deck, a job site, or driving the Glenn with the window down.

### Limit 3. The line where the wrong answer is an injury does not go on the machine.

La Crosse pointed this at the non-emergency line only; 911 goes straight to a
dispatcher (c27). **Point it at the line where a wrong route costs somebody ten
minutes, never at the line where a wrong route costs somebody a house or a
hand.** For a clinic group the machine can move a records request and must not
touch clinical triage. For a mechanical contractor the machine can handle the
invoice question and the gas-smell call goes to a person on the first word. The
hot-word list is what makes that boundary real, and a person writes it.

### Limit 4. For a lot of local operations, the honest answer is plain software.

Not a hedge, a finding. Below a few thousand routine calls a year, the licence,
the several months of preparation (c18) and the standing vendor meeting (c28)
cost more than the problem is costing you. A phone tree, published hours, an
online payment page and a text auto-reply are rung 1, testable exhaustively,
and will take the top off the same call stack.

**Do the cheap version first.** If the routine calls survive it, you have both
a real volume number and a real destination map, which are the two things a
voice build needs anyway. You will have done the data-readiness work by
accident and you will negotiate better for it.

### One qualifying question rather than a claim

This is a cloud voice product answering a phone line, so the bandwidth question
lands on your telephony, not on your camp. Judgement, not a sourced fact. Ask
what phone system it needs, whether it requires VoIP, and what the caller hears
when that link drops. For anyone with remote sites, ask it twice.
