# SELECTION — 2026-08-15 — Case File No. 2

## The pick

**The Wilson Companies** (Wilson Plumbing, Heating, Cooling & Electric Inc.),
Akron and Northeast Ohio. Residential home services. Founded 1958 by Ralph and
Theresa Wilson, third-generation family ownership, CEO John Wilson. Reported
200-plus employees.

**Bottleneck, in operator language:** the phones. Nights, weekends and overflow
calls went to an outside answering service. Raw inbound booking rate 27 percent.
Call centre at 18 CSRs.

**Build:** bought Avoca AI, an off-the-shelf home-services voice platform, and
configured it. No engineers hired, no model trained in house. The voice agent
answers, handles routine booking end to end, writes the appointment into the
CRM, and hands off to a live human CSR when it detects a frustrated caller. A
second component scores every call for coaching. They started on nights,
weekends and overflow only, then widened.

**Rung 4, WORKFLOW, honestly.** Humans designed the steps. Anything outside the
drawn path stops at a checkpoint and goes to a person. The vendor markets it as
an "AI workforce", an "AI CSR" and "agents", which is rung 5 language for a rung
4 build.

## The preference order, applied

1. **More agentic build.** Rung 4 with a real human checkpoint, and the vendor's
   own admission that full autonomy books worse. This is the best rung-4 teaching
   artifact any run has found.
2. **More ordinary company.** A family plumbing and heating outfit founded in
   1958, now run by the founders' grandson. It is difficult to be more ordinary.
3. **Better documented cost and timeline.** The only case in the bank with both,
   from the buyer's own mouth.
4. **Sector that maps hardest onto Anchorage.** Mechanical and trades
   contractors are the first segment named in `knowledge/kb/ANCHORAGE.md`, and
   mid-August is the start of heating-season prep here.

## The runner-up problem, stated honestly

**There is no runner-up.** This is a real risk to the run and it is recorded
rather than hidden.

The only other case that reached candidate quality was **Grand Island Express,
Inc.** (Grand Island, Nebraska, refrigerated truckload carrier, founded 1967,
just over 180 team members in 2021 and 164 drivers on a fresh May 2026 FMCSA
filing). Everything about it was in excellent shape after a dedicated chase:
headcount proven, timeline unusually well documented, a genuinely good named
limit, a clean rung 4 with a dispatcher veto, and strong numbers including 84
percent of loads matching the platform's recommendations from week two.

**It is DEAD ON COST**, and the chase was exhaustive. The CCJ Innovators
profile, retrieved past its 403 wall, contains the words cost, price, pricing,
paid, pay, invest, investment, ROI, dollar, subscription and contract exactly
zero times. Optimal Dynamics has no pricing page. The vendor case study is
gated. A Forbes piece on a sibling customer says the opposite of what we need,
that with load planning it is difficult to quantify the exact return on
investment.

Per filter 5 that is a hard gate with no exceptions, so it returns to the bank
as a lead with "no cost anywhere in public" named explicitly. It is a good
future case the day a cost-disclosed carrier surfaces.

**Consequence for this run:** if verification kills the Wilson cost claim, there
is no case to swap to and the run goes to the failure protocol rather than
shipping something weaker. The fact-checker has been told this explicitly, along
with the instruction that it is not a reason to pass anything thin.

## THE SIX QUESTIONS, answered honestly for the chosen case

**1. Does Dana see themselves in this company?**
Yes, more cleanly than any case we have run. A 200-person family plumbing,
heating, cooling and electrical company in Akron, founded 1958, run by the
grandson of the founders. An Anchorage mechanical contractor reads that and sees
their own shop with a different snow load.

**2. Does the problem sound like Dana's Tuesday?**
Yes. The phone ringing at 6pm with nobody to answer it, an outside answering
service taking bad messages, and a booking rate nobody in the building trusted.
This is not "inefficiency", it is the after-hours line.

**3. Can Dana picture actually doing this?**
Yes, and this is the strongest part. They bought a product, pointed it at nights
and weekends FIRST, and widened it only once it worked. We can tell Dana it ran
about 3,500 dollars a month, took two weeks to turn on and about 90 days to get
good. That is a plan, not a story.

**4. Is there a number that makes Dana sit up?**
Yes, several, and they survive being said out loud. 8,000 dollars a month to
about 3,500. Eighteen CSRs to ten while call volume grew.

**5. Does Dana learn something about AI they did not know?**
**Yes, and this is the reason to ship this case above all others.** The vendor's
own Tyson Chen said on the record that FULL autonomy books roughly 5 percent
LOWER than the hybrid human-plus-AI design. The seller argued against its own
product's autonomy. That single fact teaches the rung 4 versus rung 5 lesson
better than any explainer could: the human at the checkpoint is not a safety
blanket, it is where the money is. Dana finishes this deck able to ask a vendor
a question they could not ask before.

**6. Would Dana send this to their ops lead?**
Yes. It goes straight to whoever owns the call board.

## Honest weaknesses, carried into verification rather than glossed

1. **Independence is the weak link.** The primary source is a podcast John
   Wilson himself hosts, and episode 173 carries "Special Thanks to Avoca". The
   numbers are the host's own, about his own company, which is a real form of
   evidence, but it is not independent. Every number must ride with that label.
2. **The cost is a recollection.** Wilson says on the record that he does not
   actually know what Avoca costs, then approximates 3,500 dollars. If it ships
   it ships with the hedge he himself put on it, in the same sentence.
3. **The 30,000 dollars a month and the 18-to-10 headcount are not cleanly
   attributed to the AI alone.** Nobody on the record separates them from growth
   or other changes. Either labelled as blended, or killed.
4. **A denominator conflict that is probably a gift.** The vendor page says 85
   percent booking, the operator says thirties, for the same company. That is
   almost certainly raw inbound including abandoned and non-bookable calls
   versus bookable calls only. If the podcast explains it, we can show Dana how
   booking rates get quoted in this industry, which makes us more useful than
   either source alone. If it does not explain it, we cannot assert it.
5. **Machine transcripts are garbled** (Avoca renders as "Evoke", words cut mid
   syllable), and the fetch layer has been caught fabricating content on long
   pages this run. Every quote requires two independent renderings.
