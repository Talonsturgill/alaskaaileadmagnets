## 1. SEGMENTS

The qualifying test for this case is narrow and it is worth saying out loud, because it disqualifies most of the local market. **A segment only belongs here if it has a phone that rings after business hours with revenue on the other end of it, and a booking action that can be written into a system.** A phone that rings after hours with a complaint on the other end is a different build. A phone that rings with a quote inquiry that can wait until Tuesday is a different build too.

Ranked by strength of rhyme.

**1. Mechanical, plumbing and electrical service contractors with a residential and light commercial service division.** This is not an analogy, it is the same company in a colder building. Same bottleneck physics, the call arrives outside staffed hours and the value of the call decays in minutes. Same document stream, a work order with an address, an equipment record, a dispatch fee and a slot on the board. Same seasonality shape and a harder version of it, because the Southcentral no-heat call is not a comfort call, it is a house losing temperature with pipes in the walls. Wilson's inbound booking rate of 27 percent is the number that speaks directly to this segment, and almost nobody in it knows their own equivalent figure.

**2. Restoration, water damage and emergency response contractors.** Their entire revenue model is the after hours call. Freeze and thaw, a burst line, a failed heat trace, a flooded crawlspace. Same physics as the trades and sharper, because the caller in this segment is by definition upset, which is exactly the caller the case says should go to a person. The rhyme is strong on the intake side and weaker on the booking side, because the dispatch decision here is a judgement call about crew and equipment rather than a slot on a board.

**3. Property management and multifamily maintenance operations.** They run a published after hours emergency line by lease obligation, staffed thinly or not at all. Same document stream, a unit number, a tenant, a work order, a vendor to dispatch. Their volume is lumpy and weather driven in the same way. Distinct advantage over the trades, their after hours triage rules are usually already written down somewhere because a lease made them write it down, and written down rules are the thing the build actually needs.

**4. Home fuel and propane delivery operations serving the road system and the Valley.** A customer who runs the tank dry at 8pm in November is a revenue call, an urgent one, and the booking action is a delivery slot rather than a truck roll. Same seasonality curve, same cold snap surge, same fact that nobody wants to staff a desk for a spike that happens eleven nights a year.

**5. Towing, marine service, heavy equipment field service and aviation ground service.** After hours revenue calls with a dispatch action. Weaker rhyme on the document stream, because the intake is less standardized and the ticket size varies enormously, which changes the cost of a wrong booking.

**6. Independent clinics, dental groups, veterinary practices and urgent care.** They have the front desk load and the after hours triage line. But the cost of a wrong answer is categorically higher, the recording and consent question is real, and the correct design is triage and message capture rather than autonomous booking. Genuine rhyme on the front desk overflow, materially different design on the after hours line. Rank it here and say the difference out loud rather than lumping it in.

**7. Tour operators, lodges and charter operations.** Already on the twin map, and the phone booking load is real. Two reasons it ranks last for this case on this date. Their call is a booking inquiry, not an emergency, so the decay curve is slower and the money is smaller per call. And the calendar is wrong. As of mid August their season is running out and their staffing is falling away. Their buy window for a May season is January to March, not now.

**Segments this does not speak to, and it is worth naming them so the deck does not overreach.** Seafood processing, freight and warehousing, engineering and permitting firms, ANCSA subsidiaries running federal contracting practices. All of them have real bottlenecks. None of them has a phone that rings at 9pm with a bookable job on the line. Their answer is in a different row of the map.

## 2. THE LOCAL VERSION OF THE BUILD, in local nouns

**What it looks like now.** It is 9pm in the second week of November and the temperature dropped twenty five degrees since noon. The after hours line rings. It rolls to an answering service that is two to four time zones ahead and has never seen an Anchorage address. What lands on the dispatch board at 6.30am is a name, a callback number and the word furnace. No address, no equipment, no read on whether the house is at 40 degrees or 62. The CSR calls back at 7.15am and learns the homeowner called two more numbers at 9.20pm and somebody else's truck is already in the driveway.

**What it looks like with the front door on it.** The same call gets answered on the first ring. It asks the address, what is happening, and whether there is heat in the house at all. If it is routine work, a maintenance plan visit, a water heater, a drain that can genuinely wait until morning, it books the slot straight onto the board with the after hours dispatch fee already stated, and the work order is written before the caller hangs up. If the caller is upset, or if the house has no heat, it stops and hands the call to the on-call CSR with the address and the problem already typed. That CSR picks up already knowing what is wrong.

By 7am the next morning every call from the night before has been scored, and the CSR manager's coaching conversation is about a specific call instead of a feeling.

**The local surge that makes this different from Ohio.** Anchorage does not have a smooth overflow problem, it has a cold snap problem. The call volume is not a gentle curve, it is eleven nights a year when the temperature drops hard and every marginal furnace and boiler in town gives up inside the same six hours. You cannot staff a CSR desk for that spike, and it would be bad business to try. A voice front door absorbs the spike without hiring for the spike, and then costs you almost nothing in April. That is the whole local argument in one sentence.

**Start where Wilson started.** Nights, weekends and overflow only. Not the daytime queue. That is the copyable move and it is also the cheap one, because it puts the machine in front of the calls that are currently being handled worst, by an answering service that has no stake in whether the job gets booked.

## 3. THE HONEST RUNG

**Rung 4, workflow.** A person drew the steps. Voice in, intent sorted, routine booking completed into the field service system, and anything off the drawn path stops at a checkpoint and goes to a human. It does not decide what to do, it does what you laid out, at 9pm, without getting tired, on the eleventh call of the night.

The vendor's marketing language for this is "AI workforce," "AI CSR" and "agents." That is rung 5 vocabulary on a rung 4 build. Say so plainly and then say the part that actually helps, which is that rung 4 is very probably the right product here and the correction is about what you should pay for it, not about whether to buy it.

**And the fact that should reorganize how a local operator thinks about this whole category.** The vendor's own Tyson Chen said on the record that full autonomy books roughly 5 percent lower than the hybrid design with a human in it. The seller argued against its own product's autonomy. That is the clearest evidence this series has found that the person at the checkpoint is not a safety blanket bolted on to keep the lawyers calm. **The person at the checkpoint is where the money is.** Every rung of the ladder above 4 costs you booking rate in this application, and the company selling rung 5 language said so.

The Southcentral design instruction that falls out of that is specific. **Set the handoff threshold low, and set it lower during a cold snap.** The emotional caller in Anchorage in January is a homeowner with no heat and a kid in the house, and that is also your highest ticket call of the night. That is the last call you want a machine finishing.

### Where the honest local answer is NOT AI

This matters more here than in Akron, and it is the most useful paragraph in this dossier.

**If your after hours line takes fewer than roughly a dozen calls on a bad night and two on a normal one, you do not need this.** Rung 1, ordinary software and a written procedure, gets you most of the value for a fraction of the money. Specifically, a written intake script your existing answering service is actually required to follow that captures address, equipment and whether there is heat, plus a phone tree that texts the on-call tech directly, plus an online booking page that works on a phone at 9pm. That is a few weeks of somebody's attention and no monthly platform bill. A shop with eight techs that buys a voice platform for three after hours calls a week has bought a very good solution to a problem it does not have.

**The middle rung nobody offers you.** If the volume is real but not large, rung 3 is available and cheap. Keep a person answering, and use one model call to turn each after hours voicemail into a drafted work order with the address and equipment pulled out. One place it can be wrong, a person checks it, most of the value, a fraction of the cost and none of the integration project.

**And the free move that comes before any of it.** Pull last winter's after hours recordings and count how many became booked jobs. Wilson's number was 27 percent. Nearly three out of four callers did not become work. You can get your own version of that number this week and it costs nothing but somebody's afternoon. If the number turns out fine, you just saved yourself a purchase. If it turns out to be 27 percent, you now have the only figure that makes any of the rest of this a business decision instead of a technology decision.

## 4. COST CLASS

**The Lower 48 anchor, reported and provisional.** In the Wilson case the platform ran roughly 3,500 dollars a month and replaced an answering service running roughly 8,000 dollars a month, with about two weeks to onboard and about 90 days to get it working well. **Those are Ohio figures at an 18 CSR call centre, they are the company's own numbers, and they are not your quote.**

**ANALYSIS, ESTIMATE, not a fetched fact.** For a 60 to 250 person Southcentral service operation putting a voice front door on nights, weekends and overflow only, the honest shape is a low four figure monthly run cost, call it roughly 1,500 to 5,000 dollars a month, landing toward the top of that band only if you widen it onto the full daytime queue rather than the after hours tail. That is an annual commitment in the four to five figure range, not six. Setup is configuration work rather than engineering, so the one time number is small relative to the run cost, and the real setup expense is your CSR manager's hours writing the scripts and listening to calls.

**The assumptions, stated, because the range is worthless without them.**

- **Usage priced, so model it on January, not April.** This is the single most important pricing note for a seasonal Southcentral operation and it is the trap that catches everyone. Your bill tracks call minutes. If you build the business case on a shoulder month you will be surprised in February. Ask the vendor to price your worst cold snap week, not your average.
- **The offset is not headcount.** Wilson went from 18 CSRs to 10. A 240 person Anchorage mechanical contractor probably runs three to six CSRs and cannot subtract eight of them. Your offset is the answering service invoice, the overtime on the on-call rotation, and the jobs currently walking out the door at 9.20pm. Assume the third one is the largest and go measure it.
- **The fourth cost nobody quotes.** Change. Somebody listens to calls every week for the first quarter and tunes the script. Budget that hour, it is the difference between the deployment working and the deployment existing.
- **Alaska freight premium, none.** This is one of the very few builds in this series with no barge in it and no hardware to fly north. The product is a phone number and some configuration, so the platform costs what it costs in Ohio. The labour it offsets is priced at Alaska rates, which makes the offset side larger here than in Akron. That is an assumption to check against your own payroll, not a fetched fact.

## 5. WHAT IT TAKES

**Data readiness, in local terms, and the good news is you already have most of it.**

- **A field service system the vendor can write into.** This is the gate question and it should be asked before the demo, not after. Not "can it book appointments," but "does it write the work order into the system my dispatch board already runs on, or does it hand my CSR a message to retype." If your dispatch runs on a homegrown database or a whiteboard and a spreadsheet, the write step is the whole project.
- **Your after hours policy, written down.** What counts as an emergency, what waits until morning, what the after hours dispatch fee is, and which jobs the on-call tech will and will not take at 11pm. If that lives in one dispatcher's head, that gap **is** the project, and closing it is worth doing whether or not you ever buy anything.
- **A price book and a dispatch fee that are actually current.** The machine will quote whatever you gave it, at 9pm, to everyone.
- **Last winter's call recordings.** Your baseline. No baseline, no before and after, no way to know in March whether you bought anything.

**People, and this is where these die.** The owner does not own this. The CSR manager or the dispatch supervisor owns it, by name, with the listening time on their calendar. They tune the script, they set the handoff threshold, they run the weekly coaching off the call scores. A tool that nobody's job includes is a tool that is quietly turned off in the second quarter, and that is the most common failure in this entire category.

**A realistic ramp.** Two weeks to onboard is plausible for a configuration build. Ninety days to working well is the honest number and it matches what this series sees across patterns. Add time if your field service system is homegrown.

### The timing, and this is the sharpest locally useful thing in the case

**A 90 day ramp started in mid August is working well by mid November.** That is the right side of the heating season, which is to say the script is tuned and the handoff threshold is calibrated before the first hard cold snap rather than during it. The same 90 days started in December means you are writing call scripts in the exact week they needed to already work, on the worst nights of the year, with your CSR desk underwater.

The same arithmetic runs the other direction for the seasonal segments. A tour operator or lodge buying for a May season starts in January. A processor is not in this row at all.

## 6. WHERE IT DOES NOT TRANSLATE

At least one is required. There are six here and every one of them is real.

**1. The volume gap, and it is the big one.** Wilson ran an 18 seat call centre and cut it to 10 while volume grew. That headcount arithmetic does not exist at Southcentral scale. Most local shops in this segment run a handful of CSRs and an after hours queue measured in a dozen calls on a bad night. If the deck implies a local operator will cut eight CSRs, the deck is lying. The local case is captured revenue and absorbed spikes, not a smaller payroll, and it should be argued that way or not at all.

**2. The vendor's own 5 percent.** Full autonomy books about 5 percent lower than the hybrid, said by the seller. So the version of this that removes the person is the version that makes less money. Anyone who sells you the "AI workforce" framing is selling you the configuration their own product lead says performs worse.

**3. Integration is the whole project or it is nothing.** A national home services voice platform is built for the field service systems that dominate the Lower 48 trades market. If your dispatch runs on something local, custom or twenty years old, the booking write does not exist and you have bought a very expensive message taker. Get the integration list in writing before you spend a dollar.

**4. Vendor support hours against Alaska time.** One hour behind Pacific, four behind Eastern. An Ohio or East Coast vendor's support desk is closed by early afternoon Alaska time. When the script misroutes at 8pm on the coldest night of the year, you are alone with it until morning. Ask what the after hours escalation actually is and get an answer with a phone number in it.

**5. Connectivity, and it is narrower than you would guess.** This is a phone build, so Anchorage, the Valley, the Kenai and the road system are fine. Real time voice is latency sensitive in both directions, so if a meaningful share of your inbound comes over a satellite link from a village or a camp, test that specific path before signing rather than assuming it.

**6. The plain software answer, again, because it applies to more of this market than the AI answer does.** Below real volume the honest build is a written intake script, a phone tree that texts the on-call tech, and a booking page that works on a phone. Rung 1. Cheaper, faster, no monthly bill, and it captures a large share of what you are chasing.

**And one scoping note for the regulated segments.** The call scoring component means recording and analyzing every call. Clinics, dental and veterinary groups should settle the recording notice and consent question with their own counsel before the demo, not after the deployment. This dossier asserts nothing about Alaska recording law and no artifact should either without a fetched source.
